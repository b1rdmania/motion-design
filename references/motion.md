# Motion decisions

Give movement a purpose appropriate to the film: explanation, continuity, emphasis, atmosphere, surprise, rhythm or pleasure. Not every motion needs to explain a product feature. A showreel can legitimately foreground craft.

## Design before polish

Use paused frames to resolve composition and brand. Use a moving draft to evaluate how attention travels between them. Review early enough that changes are inexpensive, without prescribing a fixed amount of paperwork for every clip.

## Decision prompts

- What should lead the eye now? Does competing movement enrich or obscure it?
- Does the chosen acceleration suggest the right weight, mechanism or attitude? Linear travel can be appropriate; easing everything can be just as generic.
- Is essential information readable long enough? A hold may include camera travel, texture or secondary motion.
- Does the rhythm serve the emotional progression? Repetition, metronomic cuts, abrupt changes and long stillness are legitimate choices.
- Would anticipation, overlap or follow-through clarify this action, or merely decorate it?
- Does this transition express a relationship? A hard cut, dissolve, wipe, morph or continuous move can each be right.

There is no universal ban on sub-second animation, a fixed maximum amount of delight, compulsory stagger interval, or required hold/build ratio. Avoid translating a useful example into a house style.

## Match cuts

A match cut is an edit linked by a shared property such as shape, position, action or colour. It can be instantaneous. It does not require overlap, a crossfade or shared 3D geometry. State the edit mechanism separately from the visual relationship in the score. Judge the actual boundary; a pixel-difference detector cannot verify the relationship.

## Review language

Be concrete: identify the timestamp, the intended reading, what competes with it and the proposed change. “Feels generated” is not an actionable finding. Distinguish a subject-specific concept from an interchangeable template without demanding that every title card invent a unique visual language.
