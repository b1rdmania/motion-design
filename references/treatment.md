# Treatment

The treatment is the creative contract. Keep it to one page. It argues a point of view; it does not repeat the brief back.

Real directors' treatments are short and confident. "If you're seasoned, your confidence comes through in your ability to be concise" (director interview, LBB Online, "Director's Playbook: winning treatments"). Do not write "maybe".

## Template

Copy this to `plan/treatment.md` and fill every field. Mark any intake answer you assumed with *(assumed)*.

```markdown
# <film title>

## Intake
- Audience and placement:
- Remember:
- Do next:
- Feeling: <one word, or a progression a → b → c>
- Will not:

## Proposition
<one sentence, no "and">

## Tellings considered
1. <concept> — <why not / why>
2. <concept> — <why not / why>
Chosen: <n>, because <one line tied to the audience's stakes>

## Last beat
<the final image, line and sound — written before anything else>

## References
- <real work, maker, year> — take: <one thing>. Not: <what to leave>.
- ...

## Motion vocabulary
- <Verb>: <what it means in this film>
- ...

## Lead
<music | narration | visual> — <why>

## Brand
Source: <file path or URL>
Palette: <hex values>  Type: <families>  Logo: <source file>
Mandatory: <items that must be exact>

## Delivery
<formats and sizes, fps, length, loudness target, sound on/off assumption>

## Overrides
<default rule id> — <reason>

## Amendments
<date> — <what changed and why>
```

## Notes on each field

**Proposition.** It is the single most compelling thing the film must say. The Ogilvy test: "If it uses 'and', it's usually two propositions." Brand films fail "by saying three things adequately rather than one thing memorably".

**Tellings considered.** Write concepts that differ in structure, not just in style. For a bike-repair app, for example:
- one bike's week told in real time
- the mechanic's handwritten job card
- a split screen of two riders
- a countdown that runs backwards
- a feature tour (write it down so you can reject it on purpose).

The first idea is usually the category average. Buck dropped the obvious magic imagery for its David Blaine film "because as soon as you see those symbols it takes you out of yourself".

**Refusal.** A stance is defined by what it rejects. Shot on iPhone won because the winning concepts "didn't even show the iPhone" (Simon Cassels on the TBWA\Media Arts Lab pitch). A refusal can also be a claim you will not make.

**Last beat.** "Try to write the last five seconds first. If you can't, you don't need to write a spot, you need to develop a premise for a spot." (Luke Sullivan, *Hey Whipple, Squeeze This*.) Prefer a quiet close over a stack of logo, tagline and CTA all at once.

**References.** References are how a director proves taste. Name the work and the specific thing to take: "Linear, 'Introducing Linear for Agents': no VO, cut rhythm carries the argument". Also say what not to take. A reference is not a licence to imitate an unrelated brand's identity.

**Motion vocabulary.** A small set of verbs keeps motion meaningful and consistent. Example:
- Inspect: a slow, purposeful camera move that reveals where we are.
- Align: separate parts find a common position.
- Resolve: text or shape settles into one reading and stops.

If a beat's motion fits none of the verbs, question the motion.

**Tension before product.** In strong brand films the product enters as a consequence of a tension already set up, not as the subject. Airbnb's "Ellie" film opens on the viewer's own objection ("my friends said I was crazy. Why would I stay in someone else's house?") before it answers it.
