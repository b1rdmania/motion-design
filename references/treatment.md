# Treatment

The treatment is the creative contract. Keep it to one page. It argues a point of view; it does not repeat the brief back.

Real directors' treatments are short and confident: "if you're seasoned, your confidence comes through in your ability to be concise" (director interview, LBB Online, "Director's Playbook: winning treatments"). Do not write "maybe".

## Template

Copy this to `plan/treatment.md` and fill every field. Mark intake answers you assumed with *(assumed)*.

```markdown
# <film title>

## Intake
- Audience and placement:
- Remember:
- Do next:
- Feeling: <one word, or a progression a → b → c>

## Proposition
<one sentence: one central idea>

## Tellings considered
1. <concept>: <why not / why>
2. <concept>: <why not / why>
Chosen: <n>, because <one line tied to the audience's stakes>
(Or: "Concept supplied by the user: <concept>.")

## Refusal
- <what this film will not show or claim>

## Last beat
<the final image, line and sound, written before anything else>

## References
- <real work, maker, year>: take <one thing>. Leave <what not to take>.

## Craft
<what will make this showreel quality: the light, material, type, camera or sound decisions that lift it above a template>

## Motion vocabulary
- <Verb>: <what it means in this film>

## Lead
<music | narration | visual>: <why>

## Toolchain
- <shots or layer>: <tool>, because <reason>
- Compromises: <any, and why>

## Brand and assets
Manifest: plan/assets.json · Source: <file or URL>
Mandatory: <items that must be exact>

## Delivery
<formats and sizes, fps, length, loudness target, viewing width, sound on or off>

## Review path
<full path, or light path and why>

## Amendments
<date>: <what changed and why>
```

Routine implementation choices do not need an amendment. Changes to agreed timing, copy or commitments do.

## Notes on each field

**Proposition.** The single most compelling thing the film must say. The Ogilvy test, "If it uses 'and', it's usually two propositions", catches two ideas pretending to be one. It does not forbid a conjunction that names one condition. Brand films fail "by saying three things adequately rather than one thing memorably".

**Tellings considered.** Write concepts that differ in structure, not only in style. For a bike-repair app:
- one bike's week told in real time
- the mechanic's handwritten job card
- a split screen of two riders
- a countdown that runs backwards
- a feature tour (write it down so you reject it on purpose).

The first idea is usually the category average. Buck dropped the obvious magic imagery for its David Blaine film "because as soon as you see those symbols it takes you out of yourself". If the user supplied the concept, record it and move on.

**Refusal.** A stance is defined by what it rejects. Shot on iPhone won because the winning concepts "didn't even show the iPhone" (Simon Cassels on the TBWA\Media Arts Lab pitch). A refusal can also be a claim you will not make.

**Last beat.** "Try to write the last five seconds first. If you can't, you don't need to write a spot, you need to develop a premise for a spot" (Luke Sullivan, *Hey Whipple, Squeeze This*). A quiet close usually beats a stack of logo, tagline and CTA.

**References.** References are how a director proves taste. Name the work and the specific thing to take: "Linear, 'Introducing Linear for Agents': no VO, cut rhythm carries the argument". Also say what to leave. A reference is not permission to imitate an unrelated brand. Do not cite a work you have not seen.

**Craft.** Name the decisions that will make it reel-worthy: "a single slow dolly through real fog in Blender, type composited razor-sharp over it". Adjectives ("premium", "sleek") do not count.

**Motion vocabulary.** A few verbs keep motion meaningful and consistent:
- Inspect: a slow, purposeful camera move that reveals where we are.
- Align: separate parts find a common position.
- Resolve: text or shape settles into one reading and stops.

**Tension before product.** In strong brand films, the product enters as a consequence of a tension already set up. Airbnb's "Ellie" film opens on the viewer's own objection ("my friends said I was crazy. Why would I stay in someone else's house?") before it answers it.
